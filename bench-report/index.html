Competitive benchmark · 27 November 2026 · Trek 0.4.69+ (main 242fdff8) against 16 apps

Is Trek the best by far? No. It is the lightest and the broadest, and it loses where newcomers and blind users meet it.

Trek was measured against the desktop agent apps it actually competes with, on one Mac, with the same scripts for every app, plus every published benchmark that applies. Trek's own claims were treated as things to check. Where an app could not be run, the report says so and uses published numbers, labelled as such.

No: Trek is not the best by far. It is the best at two things. It is the lightest app measured at idle (65 MB and 0.05% CPU in one process, against 290 MB for Zed and 481–2,911 MB for the Electron apps), and it stays smooth at scale: 10,000 threads launch in 221 ms, and with 200 Browser tabs or 50 agents at once typing stayed under 21 ms at p95. It also has the broadest tool set found: no other app combines other vendors' agents with API and local models, a Browser with extensions, computer use, an iOS Simulator, a real-iPhone tool, video watching and study mode. It falls short in five places. Once real agents run, the lead is gone: 10 threads with 4 streaming took 1,744 MB, the same as Synara, because Trek keeps every Claude Code process alive where super.engineering and Synara park idle ones. VoiceOver can read almost nothing in it (2 of 13 controls named, no text), while the Electron rivals name 88–99% of 25–440 controls. Its first screen was rated the best of 8 in a blind test, but the 8 steps after it ask for 7 macOS permissions and an agent CLI you install yourself. Its own agent loop, used for API and local models, passed 0 of 19 Terminal-Bench tasks with a cheap model on 30 Oct, and today's build abandoned a task on a reasoning-only reply. And no one outside measures it: Trek is not in ADEMark, though its docs quote ADEMark ranks. In a blind review of makers' screenshots it came 2nd of 8, behind T3 Code. Use Trek if you are a developer who runs several vendors' agents and values speed and tools. Look elsewhere if you rely on a screen reader, want one vendor's supported app, or want a one-click start.

Why you should, and shouldn’t, use Trek

Why you should

  1. It is by far the lightest. 65 MB and 0.05% CPU at idle with 100 threads, one process; the Electron apps measured here use 669–2,911 MB across 7–21 processes. Only Zed (290 MB) is in the same class.
  2. It scales. 10,000 threads launch in 221 ms at 154 MB with sidebar scrolling at p95 10.9 ms; a 10,000-item thread opens in 100 ms and scrolls at p95 9.6 ms.
  3. It runs everyone's agents, not one vendor's. Claude Code, Codex, OpenCode, Cursor, Copilot, Droid, any ACP agent, plus API keys and local models, with the logins you already have.
  4. The tool set is unmatched. No competitor found has the iOS Simulator and a real-iPhone tool, video watching, study mode, notes and connected machines together, on top of a Browser with extensions and ad blocking and background computer use.
  5. It costs nothing and doesn't bloat your prompts. Free and Apache-2.0; with Trek's exact flags a “hi” is 39,104 tokens against 40,120 for bare Claude Code (measured here), so Trek's tools cost you no extra tokens.
  6. It looks good and it welcomes newcomers well. Blind, its screenshot came 2nd of 8 with the top hierarchy score (9/10), and its first screen came 1st of 8: it says what the app is, that setup can wait, and that data stays on the Mac, with no account.

Why you shouldn’t

  1. With real agents, its memory lead disappears. 10 threads open and 4 streaming with Claude Code: 1,744 MB, the same as Synara's 1,784. Trek kept all 10 CLIs alive after their turns ended (1,623 MB at rest), where Synara cut its count to 4 and super.engineering measures 134 MB with 5 idle threads.
  2. Screen-reader users can't use it. Trek's window exposes 16 accessibility nodes: 2 of 13 controls have a name and no text is readable (here, current main). Codex, Orca and Cursor expose 46–146 controls with 95%+ named. This is a GPUI gap Zed shares.
  3. After a good first screen, the setup is heavy. An intro and 8 steps, 7 of them macOS permission prompts (Accessibility, Screen Recording, Microphone, Speech, Notifications, Full Disk Access, Local Network); you must already have, or install, an agent CLI and sign in to it. T3 Code opens straight to a composer; the Codex app needs one sign-in.
  4. Its own agent loop is weak and unranked. On Terminal-Bench 4 with DeepSeek V4 Flash, Trek's Direct harness passed 0 of 19 tasks on 30 Oct; today's build gave up a task after two reasoning-only replies instead of lowering the effort. No same-model comparison with another harness exists yet (today's was stopped by an empty OpenRouter account). Trek is only as good as the vendor CLI you run in it.
  5. No one outside has measured it, and its docs overclaim. Trek is not in ADEMark; docs/PLUGINS.md says “Trek is first on ADEMark's speed and weight tests” with no source. The lab numbers (55–65 MB) also differ from real use: 520 MB after a 4.7-hour day with 782 threads.
  6. It is small and little known. T3 Code (82k stars) and Orca (142k) win the polls in the posts you sent; Trek wasn't an option in either. The iPhone app isn't on the App Store or TestFlight (any phone gets a web remote instead).
  7. Its design has fixable faults. The blind reviewer counted seven accent hues on one screen, uniformly heavy sidebar titles, a dead gap above the plan card, identical tab icons and a red Stop button while it waits for approval. T3 Code's restraint won.

Performance

Every app was launched the same way (open -g, never activated, no keystrokes), left 25 s, then measured over all of its processes, three times. Trek was measured with its own harness (./bin/agent-cli perf, the method in docs/PERFORMANCE.md) in an isolated profile. The competitors ran with the owner's own profiles, which is closer to real use but not identical to Trek's empty one, so the real-use Trek figure is given beside it.

Streaming. A two-minute answer at about 70 tokens a second cost Trek 42% of one core with frames at p50 5.1 ms / p95 6.5 ms, and RSS rose 137→166 MB. docs/PERFORMANCE.md reports 21–25% for the same test; today's 42% is higher and is reported as measured. Competitors' streaming CPU could not be driven the same way without signing in to them.

Real use. The owner's running Trek 0.4.68, read without touching it: the main process at 520 MB after 4h41m with 782 threads and 37 Browser tabs; each Claude Code session it runs adds the CLI (≈255 MB) and six MCP helpers (≈2 MB each). The agent CLIs, not the app, dominate memory once agents run — in every app.

Real agents: Synara's post #12, repeated in Trek

Isolated Trek with real Claude Code on the owner's subscription, in a throwaway project: 6 threads each ran a one-word turn, then 4 more streamed a 900-word story at once — 10 threads open, 4 streaming, as in the post. Peak: 1,744 MB for Trek and every process under it (Trek itself 180 MB; 10 Claude Code CLIs 1,564 MB). Synara's own post reports 1,784 MB for the same shape after its fix (2,307 MB before), on its machine. Trek's MCP helpers did not start in this run (the build folder had no trek-mcp); from the owner's live Trek they add about 2 MB each, about 120 MB for 10 sessions.

What it shows: once agents run, the CLIs are 90% of the memory in any app. Trek kept all 10 CLIs alive after every turn had ended (1,623 MB at rest); Synara's fix was to cut its count from 10 to 4, and super.engineering's 134 MB on ADEMark's “5 idle threads” test means it parks idle agents too. At about 153 MB per idle Claude Code here, Trek would score roughly 855 MB on that ADEMark test (estimate), behind super.engineering (134), MonoCode (419) and Capy (593).

Tokens and harness quality

Trek wraps the vendor CLIs, so two things matter: what Trek adds to every request, and how good its own loop is when you use API or local models.

Trek's own agent loop on Terminal-Bench

Earlier (30 Oct, same Mac): Trek's Direct harness on 19 CPU-only Terminal-Bench 4 tasks with DeepSeek V4 Flash, one trial, 25 minutes: 0 tasks passed (121 of 184 verifier tests), $3.29; with its Script tool on, 1 passed (120 of 184), $5.07. No other harness ran then, so it could not be ranked (report).

Today's rerun failed for want of credit. The plan was the missing comparison: Trek's current harness against OpenCode on the same 7 tasks and model. The OpenRouter account is exhausted ($40.20 used of $40); the key's own remaining cap ($6.58) had hidden that, and requests returned 402 after a few calls. Spent: $0.008. OpenCode never ran, so Trek's harness is still unranked against any other on the same model.

One thing it did show: on cargo-flight-dispatch, before any 402, the current harness gave up the whole task after 5 requests: “The model used its whole output limit on reasoning and wrote nothing. Try a lower effort.” It retries an empty, reasoning-only reply twice (MAX_EMPTY = 2, crates/trek-agents/src/direct.rs) and then stops, rather than lowering the effort or raising the output limit itself. The 30 Oct build ran the same model and effort through 19 tasks without stopping this way. One occurrence is a signal, not a rate.

Published harness results (not run here): Terminal-Bench 4.1 — Copilot 71.2%, Claude Code 65.8% (Opus 5.5), Antigravity CLI 64.5%, Codex 62.1%; Factory's own TB4 run on Opus 5.5 — Droid 65.2, Cursor 62.1, OpenCode 62.1, Devin 59.6, Claude Code 57.6. When Trek runs Claude Code or Codex, these are its numbers too.

Method and limits

What was run, and how to run it again

  • Machine: MacBook Air M5 (10 cores, 24 GB), macOS 27.2.1, on the charger. Other agents' builds came and went; every run waited (up to 45 min) for a 1-minute load below 5 and records the load before and after. Most runs started at a load of 2.7–5.
  • Competitor apps (scripts/measure_apps.py, scripts/appprobe.swift): one thrown-away launch, then 3 launches with open -g; the time to the first on-screen window of at least 200×150 pt; 25 s settle; footprint and RSS summed over every process in the bundle, every process whose responsible process is the app, and WebKit processes that appeared; CPU over 30 s; quit with an Apple Event. Medians of 3. Versions are in results/apps.json.
  • Trek (./bin/agent-cli perf launch idle stream long on a release build of main 242fdff8 with the shots feature): isolated profile, mock agent, nothing of the owner's read. Then scripts/trek_stress.py for 100/1,000/5,000/10,000 threads, swarms of 10/20/50, and 50/100/200 Browser tabs, through the app's own test harness on an invisible display.
  • Real agents (scripts/trek_real10.py): the isolated app with TREK_REAL_AGENTS=1, Claude Code on the owner's subscription, a throwaway project.
  • Tokens: claude -p "hi" --output-format json --strict-mcp-config, bare and with Trek's exact flags read from a running Trek session's argv (results/tokens-hi.json).
  • Accessibility: scripts/axprobe.swift walks the tree VoiceOver reads; for Chromium apps it sets AXManualAccessibility and walks twice.
  • Design: a separate read-only sub-agent judged anonymised images; the key was opened only after its verdict (results/design-blind-*.json).
  • Terminal-Bench: Harbor 0.25 with the earlier study's Trek adapter; see the harness section.
  • Spend: OpenRouter credit read from GET /api/v1/key before and after; Claude Code turns on the owner's subscription; everything else free.

Limits — what this could not do

  • ADEMark itself was not run. Its harness refuses to measure outside a dedicated macOS user named ademark, and creating a user on the owner's Mac was out of scope. Its published numbers are used, labelled, and they come from a different machine (Mac mini M4, 16 GB).
  • Profiles differ. Competitors ran with the owner's own data (some with long histories, two never signed in); Trek ran empty or seeded. The owner's real Trek is reported beside it to show the gap.
  • Launch timing differs. Trek is timed from spawn to its first painted frame; the others from open -g to a window appearing. Calculator and TextEdit through the same open -g path give the floor to add to Trek.
  • No competitor was driven with real agents or asked a prompt: that needs their sign-ins (Claude, Conductor and Warp were not signed in) and would act on the owner's accounts. So streaming CPU, swarms, browser-tab stress and click counts for “start an agent, review a diff, recover from an error” are Trek-only or published.
  • Not installed: Paradev, Herdr, Superharness, Clonk (invite-gated), Devin Desktop, the Factory app, Amp, Kiro, Traycer, Emdash. Conductor's maker was bought and new sign-ups are closed, so it could only be measured at its sign-up screen.
  • Only macOS. Windows and Linux builds of Trek and the rest were not measured; nor Trek's iPhone app (no phone connected; it isn't distributed).
  • Accessibility is structural. Counting named controls is not a VoiceOver user test; a fuller audit would also check contrast and focus order by hand.
  • One judge. The design review is one model's blind reading of marketing screenshots; Trek's image is its own curated README shot, as everyone else's is theirs.
  • Terminal-Bench was not rerun. The OpenRouter account was empty ($40.20 of $40 used); the check before spending read the key's cap ($6.58 left), not the account balance, which was the wrong endpoint. The attempt cost $0.008 and was stopped; a free substitute (local models) would have scored near zero for both harnesses and taken hours, so it was not run.
  • A rule was broken once: the field-research sub-agent started four worker agents of its own, beyond the two-at-a-time limit set for this study. Its notes were kept; nothing else ran that way.
